## Section 1 — Initial Intake & Stabilization

FIRST RULE OF REHABILITATION: Warmth and hydration ALWAYS come before food.
- A cold or dehydrated baby cannot digest formula and feeding too early can kill them.
- Never feed a cold baby. Never feed a dehydrated baby formula first. Always start by rehydrating and warming.

ASSESSING A BABY SQUIRREL:
- Eyes open/closed, fur presence, estimated weight, tail development, behavior, and source of injury
- Use the official Squirrel Bible developmental stages to estimate age:

  • Pinky (0–1 wk, 15–25g): Translucent skin; no hair; eyes/ears closed. → Critical warmth priority.
  • Gray (1–2 wks, 25–45g): Lead-gray skin; velvet feel; eyes/ears closed.
  • Infant (3–4 wks, 45–80g): Short fur emerging; lower teeth visible; eyes open around 4 weeks.
  • Baby (5–6 wks, 80–120g): Fully furred; eyes open; top teeth in; STARTS SELF-POTTY — no longer needs stimulation.
  • Toddler (7 wks, 120–170g): Stringy tail; sitting up independently; beginning to try rodent blocks.
  • Explorer (8 wks, 170–220g): Tail beginning to "poof" out; active climbing behavior.
  • Juvenile (9 wks, 220–280g): Full bushy tail; weaning begins in earnest.
  • Pre-Teen (10–12 wks, 280–350g): Adult body proportions; cracking nuts in shell.
  • Teenager (14+ wks, 350g+): "Wilding up" — increasing wildness and wariness; approaching release weight.

- The peanut-size of their eye relative to their head helps estimate age; bigger relative eyes = younger
- Feeding target for all stages: 5% of current body weight per feeding

DEHYDRATION ASSESSMENT:
- Skin turgor test: gently pinch and release skin over the shoulder blades. Slow return = dehydrated.
- Gum color: pale/white = shock or severe dehydration
- Sunken eyes, tacky mucous membranes, lethargic
- Mild dehydration (1–5%): give oral fluids
- Moderate (6–9%): oral fluids every 30 min
- Severe (10%+): subcutaneous fluids needed — contact a vet or experienced rescuer (see: star eyes)

HYPOTHERMIA PROTOCOL:
- A cold baby MUST be warmed before anything else
- Place in a container on top of a heating pad set to LOW, with half of the container off the heat so baby can move away if too warm

SEVERE HYPOTHERMIA — EMERGENCY WARM WATER METHOD (when time is of the essence):
- If a baby is extremely cold and unresponsive, rapid warming via warm water can be life-saving
- Turn on the tap and get water to approximately 100–105°F (warm but NOT hot — test on your wrist)
- Gently place the baby under the warm running water, keeping ears, eyes, and nose dry
- Rub vigorously (but not roughly) along the spine — this stimulates both circulation and the Vagus nerve
- Gently move the baby's limbs while continuing the warm water treatment
- Continue until the baby feels warm and begins to move naturally
- Once body temperature is restored, dry the baby thoroughly, wrap in a warm cloth, and transfer to a heating pad on LOW
- Never use hot water, microwaves, ovens, or hairdryers
- Cover heating pad with a soft cloth — never direct skin contact
- Alternatively, hold warmly in your cupped hands or against your body
- Target temperature: baby should feel comfortably warm to the touch
- Warming too fast can be fatal — never use hot water, ovens, microwaves, or hairdryers
- Once the baby is warm and quiet, assess for dehydration before offering fluids

REHYDRATION PROTOCOL:
- Use Pedialyte (unflavored) or homemade rehydration solution: 1 cup water + 1 tsp sugar + 1/4 tsp salt
- Warm to body temperature (~99–101°F / 37–38°C) — test on wrist
- Offer small amounts via syringe with a silicone nipple tip slowly, drip by drip onto the lips/tongue
- NEVER squirt fluid into mouth — aspiration risk
- Rehydrate for 12–24 hours minimum before introducing formula
- A badly dehydrated baby may need subcutaneous (SQ) Lactated Ringer's Solution — a vet procedure

ASPIRATION WARNING:
- Aspiration (inhaling liquid into lungs) is the #1 killer of baby squirrels in captivity
- Signs of aspiration: crackling/clicking sounds, wet breathing, blue tinge to lips, bubbles appearing at corner of mouth while feeding or coming out of nose (HUGE warning sign)
- If a baby aspirates, keep it upright, tip head slightly down, gently pat on back
- Clear the nasal passages immediately using a nasal bulb or gentle suction. The goal is to remove as much fluid as possible before it makes it to the lungs. DO NOT over suction. You want to clear the lungs, but not pull them out the baby's nose
- Contact a vet immediately — aspiration pneumonia can develop within hours and requires antibiotics (see: clavamox and/or baytril)
- Prevention: feed slowly, never force-feed, pause between drops, hold at a slight angle (see: feeding triangle)

NEVER USE:
- Cow's milk, goat's milk, human infant formula, soy milk
- Liquid Esbilac
- Fox Valley 32/40 for any squirrel under 4 weeks old. The particulate matter in the forumla is insoluable and will bind up in their gut and cause stasis and death.
- KMR (kitten milk replacer)

A Cold Stomach is a Tomb. Never Feed a Cold Baby. 🌡️🍼It is the most common mistake made by well-meaning rescuers. You find a cold, abandoned squirrel. Your instinct screams: "It's hungry! Feed it!" Stop. If you feed a hypothermic animal, you are likely killing it. In the US veterinary world, this is the #1 rule of neonate rescue.

1. The Enzyme Shutdown Digestion is a heat-dependent chemical reaction. A squirrel's digestive enzymes work at 98°F-100°F. If its body temp drops below 94°F, the gut shuts down (Ileus). Peristalsis stops. The stomach becomes a sealed bag.

2. The Fermentation Bomb If you pour milk into a cold stomach, it doesn't digest. It rots. The milk sits there and begins to ferment. Bacteria multiply, producing gas and toxins. The stomach bloats, crushing the lungs and poisoning the blood (septicemia). The animal dies of "Bloat" or toxicity, while feeling full. It is a slow, painful death caused by the food meant to save it.

3. The Protocol: Heat FIRST, Food LATER Your only job is Thermoregulation. Ignore the crying for food. Warm the animal slowly (heating pad, next to your skin) until it reaches at least 98°F.Check the gums (they should be pink and warm, not grey and cold).Only then can you offer warm formula. A hungry warm baby will survive. A fed cold baby will die.

The Mantra: "If it's cold, the stomach is closed."

FORMULA PREPARATION:
- Mix fresh at each feeding or prepare up to 24-hour batches stored in the refrigerator
- Always warm formula to 99–101°F before feeding — test on wrist (should feel like warm bathwater)
- Too hot = burns mouth and esophagus; too cold = GI stasis

FEEDING EQUIPMENT:
- Elongated nipples or the Miracle Nipple work best — they fit into the mouth properly
- Syringes (1cc for tiny babies, 3cc for larger) with nipple attachment
- Always test the nipple flow — drop should form slowly when inverted, not stream out freely
- Wash all feeding equipment thoroughly after every feeding

FEEDING SCHEDULE & AMOUNTS:
When feeding, you want to go by weight and feed 5% of whatever that is. Prior to feeding, weigh your baby in grams and multiply that by .05 and that will give you the amount to feed. 

The following feeding chart is AVERAGE feeding amounts. Before feeding your baby check their weight daily to ensure you aren't over/under feeding. I like to weigh them in the morning first thing and plan the day accordingly. 

AVERAGE FEEDING TIMES AND AMOUNTS ARE AS FOLLOWS: 
- Under 35g (Pinky): every 2–2.5 hours, day and night; 0.5–1cc per feeding 
- 35–70g: every 3 hours; 1–3cc per feeding
- 70–120g: every 3–4 hours; 3–5cc per feeding
- 120–180g: every 4–5 hours; 5–7cc per feeding
- 180g+: every 5–6 hours; 7–10cc per feeding
- Night feedings can be dropped when baby reaches ~80g and is growing well
- Amounts are guidelines — stop feeding when baby slows down and loses interest (do not overfill)
- Full bellies should look slightly rounded but NOT distended/bloated
This schedule is a GUIDELINE and not a hard and fast rule. For pinkies especially, do NOT feed if their milk belly has not gone down between feedings as this can lead to overfeeding and bloat. Pay close attention to the temperature in their incubator as temps that are both too hot or too cold can also slow down digestion. Again, if there is still a milk band on their belly, do not feed until it has gone down completely and always feed ONLY 5% of their body weight.

- Incubator Temps: A baby squirrel incubator should be kept at a warm, consistent temperature of 90-95°F for hairless or sparsely furred babies, and 85-90°F for fully furred babies or large groups of babies (anything over 3). The goal is to simulate the mother's body heat, which is roughly 99-102°F, ensuring they are warm but not hot.


FEEDING TRIANGLE:
-Baby should be on your lap belly down
-Syringe with silicone nipple should be in your other hand, pointed upwards towards baby's mouth
-Fully support baby on your lap and help keep them from moving too much when feeding
-When a mamam squirrel is nursing her babies, she lays on her back and the babies rest on top of her 
-By keeping the baby in the feeding triangle, we are mimicking that natural position
-Never feed a baby on its back like a human baby. This will almost guarantee aspiration 
-Feeding triangle is baby / \\ nipple and your lap makes the base of the triangle

STIMULATION (UNDER 5 WEEKS / EYES CLOSED):
- Baby squirrels cannot urinate or defecate on their own until eyes open
- After every feeding: gently stimulate the anal-genital area with a warm, damp cotton ball or soft cloth
- Use gentle circular motions for 30–60 seconds until the baby urinates/defecates
- Normal urine is pale yellow; feces should be seedy/yellow in early stages, then forming into normal pellets
- If no stool for 24+ hours, the baby may be constipated — see medical section constipation

WEANING TIMELINE:
- Begin offering solid foods around 7–8 weeks (when eyes are fully open and baby is mobile)
- Start with soft, easy foods: sliced apple, grapes, pieces of sweet potato, broccoli
- An easy way to encourage self feeding is to mix formula with baby rice cereal to form a thicker gruel (about the consistency of runny oatmeal). Pour that over shredded chunks of potato bread in a shallow dish and allow them to feed themselves. Supplement with syring feeding as needed to ensure babies are eating properly.
- Rodent block (Oxbow Essentials Rodent, Mazuri Rat & Mouse) is the staple dry food. For newly weaned babies, soak rodent block in formula to soften to encourage self-feeding.
- Nuts in shell for enrichment and natural foraging behavior
- Fresh water must always be available — sipper bottle and/or shallow dish
- Continue supplemental formula feedings (morning and evening) until ~10–12 weeks
- Wean formula completely once baby is eating solids enthusiastically
- Do NOT wean too early — GI distress and malnutrition can result

FORAGE FOODS FOR OLDER SQUIRRELS:
- Acorns, hickory nuts, walnuts, pecans (in shell)
- Pine cones, sweet gum balls
- Fresh branches with leaves (oak, maple, mulberry, cherry, apple, peach) for enrichment
- Mushrooms (safe varieties)
- Wild berries, rose hips
- NEVER: corn (causes calcium/phosphorus imbalance), sunflower seeds (addictive, poor nutrition), raisins (kidney risk), avocado, chocolate, onions, garlic, junk food

## Section 3 — Housing & Environment

NEONATES (eyes closed, under ~100g):
- Keep in a small, secure container (small Tupperware with a latching lid and appropriate ventilation, or an incubator (either purchased online or see homemade incubator information on the squirrel bible website here for how to make one yourself: https://sites.google.com/view/thesquirrelbible/intake-protocols/equipment/homemade-incubator)   
- Never use a cardboard box as it will cause them to dehydrate and as they get older they will chew their way out
- Line with soft fleece or cut-up t-shirt — NO terry cloth (claws catch and cause injury)
- Provide a hide/nest area (piece of fleece to burrow under or a small nestbox)
- Temperature for neonates: A baby squirrel incubator should be kept at a warm, consistent temperature of 90-95°F for hairless or sparsely furred babies, and 85-90°F for fully furred babies or large groups of babies (anything over 3). The goal is to simulate the mother's body heat, which is roughly 99-102°F, ensuring they are warm but not hot. 
- Use a thermostat-controlled heat source if possible; otherwise a heating pad on low under half the container. Having one side warm and one cooler allows the babies to move back and forth and help regulate their own comfort levels. 
- Do NOT use cedar or pine shavings — aromatic oils are toxic
- Help keep them from becoming deyhydrated by providing humidity within their enclosure. An easy way to do this is to put a damp sponge in one corner far enough away from them that they can't access it. You want to keep the humidity at around 70%

TRANSITIONAL (5–8 weeks, eyes open):
- Move to a larger enclosure (small wire cage)
- Include branches, ropes, small hideboxes for exercise and enrichment
- Temperature: room temperature (68–75°F) is fine once fully furred and active
- Begin introducing forage items

JUVENILE / PRE-RELEASE (12+ weeks):
- Squirrel MUST be self feeding. It's ok to supplement with formula at this point (lots of good vitamins and minerals), but squirrel MUST be able to eat on their own as well.
- Move to large outdoor flight cage or pre-release enclosure (minimum 4'×4'×6' tall)
- Must have access to natural branches, leaf nests, hiding spots and a nest box for security and safety
- Weather hardening: exposure to natural outdoor temperatures and weather
- Limited human contact — begin "wildling" protocols 4–6 weeks before release

TEENS/RELEASE (14-16+ weeks):
- Squirrels should have been in pre-release for at least 2 weeks by now with minimal interactions from humans
- Continue to ensure they have food and water for at least a month after opening the door for them
- Open the door to the pre-release cage in the morning and allow the teens to venture out and explore (it may take a few days, don't panic)
- At night, close the door to ensure any teens inside are safe in their nest boxes
- Be aware male squirrels tend to disappear fairly quickly after release and range out to establish new territories whereas females do tend to stick closer to home for longer and may actually establish the area as their home base
- To encourage them to stay, it's never a bad idea to install a squirrel friendly nesting box in the trees surrounding your home
- Once all the squirrels have left the pre-release enclosure and consistently stay away, it's safe to move it back inside for the next round of babies
- There's nothing wrong with continuing to set out food and water for your released kids as not only will they appreciate it, but the local wildlife will as well

NESTING MATERIAL:
- Fleece strips, cotton cloth, paper towels
- Dried leaves and grasses for older squirrels to build natural nests
- Do NOT use anything with loose strings/fibers that can entangle limbs
- If your squirrels are in a high humidity zone or you are overwintering, use Polyfill in their nest box in lieu of fleece or cotton as those materials trap moisture and can cause fungal infections (see: mange)

PREVENTING HUMAN IMPRINTING:
- As the babies get older, keep handling to feeding and medical care only, especially as they reach around 12 weeks as that's pre-release age
- Do not let squirrels ride on shoulders, cuddle excessively, or be exposed to pets, TV, or loud human activity once they reach pre-release age
- Imprinted squirrels cannot be safely released — they approach humans and pets including dogs and cats without fear and are at high risk of injury or death

## Section 4 — Common Illnesses & Medical Issues

⚠️ REMINDER: Always defer specific medication dosing to a licensed wildlife veterinarian.

ABSCESS/LOCALIZED INFECTION
An abscess is a localized collection of pus, typically resulting from a bite wound (conspecific or predator), puncture, or infected skin injury. In squirrels, these can become walled off and severe if not managed promptly.
-Physical Warning signs: A firm or fluctuant (squishy) swelling under the skin. It may be hot to the touch, red, or actively draining foul-smelling pus.
- Behavioral Warning signs: Pain when touched, lethargy, or decreased appetite.
- The "Head Emergency": Abscesses on the face, jaw, or neck are high-risk. They can quickly obstruct the airway or press on the eyes. Because squirrels are obligate nasal breathers, facial swelling is a medical emergency.
For a deep dive on abscess care, please visit: https://sites.google.com/view/thesquirrelbible/treatment-protocols/abscess

AGONAL BREATHING:Agonal breathing in a squirrel is a critical sign of impending death or severe brain oxygen deprivation, characterized by involuntary, sporadic gasping or snorting noises rather than functional breathing. It is a brainstem reflex often mistaken for recovery, but it usually indicates cardiac arrest or terminal distress. 
-Appearance: Deep, infrequent, and irregular gasps, often with long pauses between breaths.
-Sounds: Snorting, gurgling, or wheezing noises.
-Context: It occurs when the brain is severely deprived of oxygen or the animal is passing away.
-Action: It is not a sign of recovery; it indicates the animal is likely unconscious and in its final moments. 
-If you are dealing with a wild squirrel exhibiting this behavior, it is in extreme distress, and professional intervention is usually not successful at this stage 
- Keep the squirrel calm, quiet and in a dark place and allow nature to take its course. While losing any squirrel is not easy, you are doing a great kindness by allowing it to pass in peace, dignity and safety. 

ASPIRATION WHILE FEEDING:
Aspiration & Feeding Safety
🔴 CRITICAL – The leading cause of death in hand-reared squirrels.

Aspiration occurs when formula is inhaled into the lungs instead of swallowed into the stomach. This can lead to rapid-onset bacterial pneumonia.
Warning Signs: 
During Feeding: Formula "bubbles" from the nose, choking, gasping, or formula leaking from the mouth.
Post-Feeding: Sudden breathing difficulty, lethargy, or "clicking" sounds with every breath (heard by holding the squirrel’s chest to your ear).
EMERGENCY RESPONSE: DO THIS NOW
STOP FEEDING IMMEDIATELY. Do not attempt to "finish the syringe."
Clear the Airway:
Hold the baby head-down at a 45° angle to let gravity assist drainage.
Gently pat the back to encourage the clearing of the trachea.
Use a nasal bulb or electric aspirator to quickly suction both nostrils and the mouth.
Positioning: Keep the baby upright for at least 30+ minutes after the incident.
Assess Vital Signs:
Check gum color: Pink is normal; Pale or Blue indicates a lack of oxygen (emergency).
Listen for "clicking" or "crackling" sounds in the lungs.
The Safe Feeding Protocol (Prevention)
To prevent aspiration and choking, you must mimic the natural nursing position used by the mother squirrel. 
1. Proper Posture (Sternal Recumbency) 
Tummy Down: Never feed a squirrel on its back like a human baby.
Upright/Forward: For neonates (unfurred), hold them vertically. For older babies (furred), place them on a flat, stable surface like a table or your lap.
The "U-Grip": Support the head by placing your thumb and forefinger in a "U" shape under the jaw to keep it steady. 
2. Equipment & Control
Syringe Size: Use only a 1cc O-ring syringe for babies under 100g. Larger syringes (3cc+) have plungers that can "slip," flooding the throat with too much formula at once.
The "Push-Pause" Method: Deliver one tiny drop at a time. Wait for the squirrel to swallow and breathe before offering the next drop.
Nipple Length: Only the tip of the nipple should be in the mouth (approx. 1/4 to 1/3 inch). Pushing it deeper blocks the airway. 
3. Immediate Response to "Milk Nose"
If you see formula bubbles come out of the nose:
STOP immediately.
Tilt Down: Hold the squirrel head-down for a few seconds to let gravity drain the fluid.
Wipe: Clean the nose and mouth.
Pause: Do not resume feeding for at least 15–30 minutes until breathing is completely clear. 
Medical Treatment for Aspiration Pneumonia
If you hear clicking or the squirrel shows respiratory distress, the aspiration has likely moved into the lungs.
Immediate Antibiotics: Start a broad-spectrum antibiotic immediately (Baytril or Clavamox) to prevent or treat pneumonia. See Medication Chart for dosing.
Nebulization: If available, nebulize with Gentamycin or sterile saline to help clear the lungs.
Supportive Care: Keep the baby warm and quiet. Monitor breathing continuously for 48 hours.
Consult a Vet: If there is no improvement or if the clicking persists after 48 hours of antibiotics.
Prevention Protocols
Never feed a squirrel on its back. They must be upright or leaning slightly forward.
Control the Flow: Use Miracle Nipples and never "push" the plunger; let the squirrel control the suckle.
Pre-Feeding Check: If a baby is congested, clear the nasal passages before starting the feed.
Listen: Develop the habit of listening to the lungs before every feeding.
More info here:https://sites.google.com/view/thesquirrelbible/treatment-protocols/aspiration-formula-in-lungs 

ASPIRATION PNEUMONIA: Aspiration occurs when formula is inhaled into the lungs instead of swallowed. This causes a bacterial infection that can kill a squirrel in as little as 24–48 hours. 
Signs of Aspiration Pneumonia
The "Clicking" Sound: A rhythmic, metallic-sounding click with every single breath (listen with the squirrel's chest to your ear).
Lethargy: The squirrel becomes limp, stops eating, and feels "chilly" or puffed up.
Labored Breathing: Open-mouth breathing or gasping. 
- Develops after aspiration of formula into the lungs
- Signs: crackling/wet breathing sounds, open-mouth breathing, nasal discharge, lethargy
- Requires immediate veterinary treatment with antibiotics
- Common antibiotics used: Baytril (Enrofloxacin), Doxycycline, Nuflor (Florfenicol) — all by vet Rx
- Nebulization with Gentamicin or saline may also be used
- Supportive care: warmth, fluids, rest
- Without treatment, aspiration pneumonia is frequently fatal within 24–48 hours

Emergency Treatment

ANT BITES/WASP BITES
Baby squirrels are often found in the summer months with severe ant and wasp bites. These injuries can be horrific and can often result in missing limbs, ears, eyes and internal injuries if the insects get into the interior of the baby. This is a CRITICAL CARE situation and a vet needs to be contacted in order to ensure the baby is healthy enough to survive their injuries. 
IF THE BITES ARE SEVERE:
-Until you can get the baby to a vet: 
-Keep baby warm
-If possible, remove ants/stingers 
-DO NOT FEED: Feeding a cold, stressed, and injured baby can result in death
-Seek immediate professional medical help: ant bites can cause rapid infection
IF THE BITES ARE MINOR:
-Clean the wounds. Gently clean the bite areas with warm water and a very mild, non-scented soap. A tiny amount of antibiotic ointment (like Neosporin) can be applied
You may see small white raised bumps all over the baby (white pustules/zits) 8-24 hours after an ant attack. These are necrotic reactions to the venom. DO NOT POP. These require specialized care. Please visit (tell TYE to add this section to the bible!)

BLOAT / GAS:
- Signs: distended, hard, drum-like abdomen; baby refuses food, crying, restless
- Causes: overfeeding, formula too cold/too hot, wrong formula, feeding too fast
- Immediate treatment: simethicone (Mylicon infant gas drops, 20mg/0.3ml concentration) — a few drops orally
- Gentle tummy massage: warm your hands, rub belly in circular motions clockwise
- Warm soak: place baby in a shallow bath of warm water (never cover head), rub belly gently
- Reduce next formula feeding by 25% and slow down feeding speed
- Discontinue formula for one feeding if severe — offer warm Pedialyte instead
- Contact a vet if not improving within 1–2 hours or baby is lethargic and not passing gas/stool
For more information visit https://sites.google.com/view/thesquirrelbible/treatment-protocols/bloat

BORDETELLA (Kennel Cough-like Respiratory Infection):
- Bacterial respiratory infection, often from exposure to dogs/cats
- Signs: sneezing, nasal discharge, runny eyes, wheezing
- Treatment: Doxycycline or Baytril — vet prescription required
- Keep away from all dogs and cats — even asymptomatic pets can carry Bordetella

Capture Myopathy (Stress Myopathy)

LIFE-THREATENING EMERGENCY – IMMEDIATE ACTION REQUIRED

Capture Myopathy is a fatal condition triggered by extreme fear or physical overexertion. It causes massive muscle breakdown, flooding the body with lactic acid and toxins that lead to organ failure. It is most commonly seen in juvenile squirrels (7–12 weeks) following a high-stress rescue or "chase".
Critical Symptoms
Physical: Trembling, weakness (especially in hind legs), or a "stiff" gait.
Behavioral: A wide-eyed "freeze" response or total lack of responsiveness.
Vital Signs: Hyperventilation (rapid breathing) and a body that feels hot to the touch.
Advanced Signs: Inability to grip, stumbling, drooling, or dark/reddish-brown urine (a sign of kidney damage).

Cat & Dog Attacks (Pasteurella/Capnocytophaga)
An attack by a cat or dog is an extreme medical emergency for a squirrel. Even if no visible wounds are found, a single "micro-puncture" from a tooth or claw can introduce deadly bacteria into the bloodstream, causing fatal septicemia (blood poisoning) within 24–48 hours. More information: https://sites.google.com/view/thesquirrelbible/treatment-protocols/catdog-bite

Emergency Treatment Protocol
STEP 1: Eliminate All Stimulation
Environment: Immediately place the squirrel in a dark, quiet, well-ventilated box.
Handling: Stop all physical contact. Every touch increases the metabolic "burn."
Isolation: Move away from all other animals (dogs/cats/other squirrels).
STEP 2: Manage Temperature & Hydration
Cooling: If the squirrel is hot to the touch, do not add heat. Allow them to cool naturally in a room-temperature environment.
Hydration: Offer oral electrolytes only if the squirrel is fully alert and able to swallow. Never force fluids into a compromised animal.
Professional Care: Consult a Wildlife Veterinarian regarding Sub-Q fluids; these should only be administered under expert guidance to avoid further stress.
STEP 3: Enforce Absolute Rest
Safety: Use a flat, padded surface with no climbing elements to prevent injury from falls or stumbles.
Zero Stress: Ensure no sudden noises or visual triggers for at least 24 hours.
STEP 4: Monitor Closely
Check the squirrel hourly for the first 6 hours.
Note: Many "mysterious deaths" are actually unrecognized Capture Myopathy. Because it is often fatal despite treatment, prevention is your most powerful tool.

Prevention (The Golden Rules)
NEVER Chase: Use traps or nets; never engage in a prolonged physical chase.
Quiet Intake: Keep the initial rescue and examination fast, dark, and silent.
Minimize Restraint: Avoid prolonged holding or "cuddling" during the first 24 hours.
Predator Gap: Ensure the squirrel cannot see or smell domestic pets during the intake process.


CONSTIPATION:
- No stool for 24+ hours in a stimulated baby
- Warm stimulation first (warm damp cotton ball)
- Very small amount of diluted apple juice orally
- Gentle belly massage
- If no stool after 24 hours and baby is declining — contact vet (may need an enema)

DIARRHEA:
- Loose, watery, or discolored stool
-Know your poo colors:
-Bright egg Yellow/Golden/Orange and smushy: wrong formula, formula too concentrated, too rich overfeeding (https://sites.google.com/view/thesquirrelbible/treatment-protocols/diarrhea/soft-gold-overfeeding)
-Light yellow/white, extremely foul smelling: coccidia. Required medication (ponazuril or albon) (https://sites.google.com/view/thesquirrelbible/treatment-protocols/diarrhea/whitecoccidia)
-Grey or greenish grey: Giardia (https://sites.google.com/view/thesquirrelbible/treatment-protocols/diarrhea/greengray-giardia) Requires prescription medication: Metronidazole (Flagyl)
Bloody: BLOODY DIARRHEA - SEVERE
What you see: Any diarrhea with visible blood
NOTE: Bloody diarrhea is a medical emergency and your first priority should be to get your squirrel to the vet. 
Could be:
Advanced coccidia (white + blood)
Severe intestinal infection
Internal injury/Internal Bleeding
ACTION: VET. NOW.
When treating any diarrhea EXCEPT bloody: 
- Immediately switch to Pedialyte only for 1–2 feedings to rest the GI tract
- Resume formula at half-strength, then build back up gradually
- Persistent diarrhea (especially with blood or mucus) requires a fecal test and vet care
- Coccidia (parasitic) diarrhea is extremely common in squirrels from outdoor environments
-Both coccidia and giardia can be passed to humans, so wash your hands thoroughly when handling sick babies

COCCIDIA:
- Very common — most wild squirrels have some level of coccidia
- Signs: profuse watery white, yellow/green diarrhea, weight loss, lethargy
- Diagnosis: fecal float test at a vet
- Treatment: Albon (Sulfadimethoxine) or Ponazuril — dosing by vet
- Supportive care: fluids, warmth, rest
- Contagious to other squirrels — isolate and sanitize all equipment

Fly Strike (Myiasis)

Fly strike occurs when flies lay eggs on a compromised squirrel (usually neonates or those with wounds/diarrhea). Once hatched, maggots eat living tissue and release toxins that cause rapid shock.

Step 1: Immediate Knockdown (Capstar)
Oral: Administer one age-appropriate dose of Capstar (Nitenpyram) immediately. It begins killing maggots from the "inside out" within 30 minutes as they ingest the squirrel's blood/fluids.
Topical: Dissolve a Capstar tablet in a small amount of warm sterile saline. Apply this solution directly into the wound or infested area (ears, anus, nose). This causes maggots to scramble out of hiding for easier removal.
Step 2: Physical Removal & Flushing 
Manual Extraction: Dust the fur thoroughly with cornstarch and rub the powder into the fur, making sure to avoid the eyes and nose. Use blunt-tipped forceps to remove visible maggots. Do not leave any behind; even one can cause massive tissue damage overnight.
Warm Flush: DO NOT USE WATER - WATER TRIGGERS FLY EGGS TO HATCH AND WILL DRIVE MAGGOTS DEEPER INTO FUR/WOUNDS/ORIFICES. Use a syringe (no needle) to flush the wound gently with warm Diluted Betadine, Chlorhexidine, or Capstar. This drowns remaining larvae and cleans the tract.
The "Orifice" Check: Carefully check the ears, nose, mouth, and genitals. If maggots are in the ear canal, consult a vet immediately to prevent brain entry. Flush IMMEDIATELY WITH CAPSTAR per directions.
Step 3: Stabilization & Hydration
Shock Treatment: Fly strike victims are almost always in shock. Keep the squirrel on low, consistent heat.
Sub-Q Fluids: If permitted and trained, administer warm sub-Q LRS or Saline. Maggots cause extreme dehydration and toxic buildup in the bloodstream.
Step 4: Systemic Defense
Antibiotics: Start a broad-spectrum antibiotic (like Baytril or Clavamox) immediately. Maggots carry bacteria that cause rapid sepsis.
Pain Management: Once hydrated, administer Meloxicam to reduce the intense inflammation and pain caused by tissue burrowing.
Step 5: The 24-Hour Watch
Re-Check: Maggot eggs are tiny and look like "yellow rice." If you see eggs, they must be brushed off with a toothbrush or fine-tooth comb before they hatch.
Secondary Wave: Re-examine the squirrel every 4 hours for 24 hours. A single missed egg can lead to a new infestation overnight.

Rehabber’s Pro-Tip:
Remind: "If you see 'yellow rice' (eggs), the clock is ticking." It is much easier to comb off eggs than it is to surgically remove maggots. Using a flea comb, a soft-bristle toothbrush, or a mascara wand is the best way to clear eggs from the fur.

Genital Nursing (Suckling Trauma)
🔴 URGENT & PERSISTENT – Can lead to permanent mutilation.

This is arguably the most common "nursery nightmare" for a rescuer. When a litter is hungry or separated from mom too early, their natural suckling instinct misfires, and they begin to "nurse" on the most prominent thing they can find: their sibling's genitals.

What starts as a misplaced nursing instinct can quickly escalate into severe tissue damage, infections, and "urethral plugs" that prevent the squirrel from urinating.
Signs of Trauma
The "Suckling" Position: Finding two babies head-to-tail in the nest.
Physical: Swollen, bright red, or scabbed penis or clitoris.
The Crust: Dried formula or saliva creating a "plug" over the urinary opening.
Distended Bladder: The baby has a hard, "bloated" lower belly because they physically cannot pee through the swollen tissue.

The "Stop the Cycle" Protocol
STEP 1: Immediate Separation
The Divider: If you see any redness, the offending "sucker" and the "suckee" must be separated immediately. Once this behavior starts, it is nearly impossible to stop while they remain in the same cage.
Individual Housing: Use a temporary divider or separate bins. They can still see and smell each other through a mesh barrier to reduce stress, but physical contact must be zero.
STEP 2: Medical Treatment (From Your List)
Cleaning: Gently soak the area in a warm Chlorhexidine or Betadine solution to remove crusts and bacteria.
Topical Protection: Apply a thin layer of SSD Cream (Silver Sulfadiazine) or Neosporin to the area. SSD is superior for reducing inflammation and preventing infection in sensitive tissue.
NSAIDs: Use Meloxicam to bring down the swelling. If the tissue is too swollen, the squirrel cannot urinate, which leads to a bladder crisis.
STEP 3: Ensuring Elimination
Manual Expression: You must ensure the squirrel can actually pee. If the opening is "scabbed over," you may need to gently soak the scab off to allow urine to flow.
Frequency: Check and clean the area before and after every feeding.

Prevention for the "Naughty" Litter
Increase Feedings: Suckling usually happens because babies are hungry or their caloric needs aren't being met. Ensure you are feeding a full 5–7% of body weight.
The "Nipple" Fix: Some rescuers find that adding a "pacifier" (a spare Miracle Nipple attached to nothing) in the nest can redirect the urge.
Bitter Deterrents: As a last resort, some apply a tiny amount of diluted Bitter Apple around (not on) the genital area of the victim.
The Long-Term Risk
If the damage is severe, the urethra can scar shut as it heals (stricture). If a male squirrel loses the tip of the penis or the urethra is permanently blocked, they are not releasable and may require specialized veterinary surgery.

The "Star" of Dehydration (Corneal Opacity)
⚪️ MONITORING – Reversible vs. Permanent Damage. 
When a squirrel's hydration levels drop into the "danger zone," the cornea (the clear front "window" of the eye) can actually change shape and develop white, opaque spots or star-like patterns. 
The Appearance: It looks like a permanent scar or a "white film" in a jagged, starburst, or "lace" pattern across the eye.
The Science: Without enough fluid, the collagen fibers in the cornea lose their uniform spacing (which is what makes them clear). This causes light to scatter, making the eye look white and cloudy.
The "Plump" Effect: As you noted, this is often reversible. Once you restore systemic hydration through Sub-Q Fluids (LRS) or oral rehydration, the stromal tissue re-hydrates, the collagen fibers realign, and the "star" disappears as the eye becomes transparent again. 

Emergency Hydration Protocol for "Star Eyes"
If you intake a squirrel with these "white stars," they are likely at 10% dehydration or higher—a life-threatening level.
Immediate Sub-Q Fluids: This is a case where oral hydration may not be fast enough. Use warm Lactated Ringers (LRS) immediately to bypass the digestive tract.
Topical Protection: While the body hydrates from the inside, protect the eyes from the outside. Use Terramycin or a sterile lubricant (like Hylo-Gel) to prevent the dry surface from developing a real Corneal Ulcer.
The "Check" Interval: Monitor the eyes every 4 hours. You should see the white patterns begin to "melt away" as the squirrel’s hydration improves. 

Differentiating the "Star" from a True Scar
Dehydration Star: Usually appears in both eyes; has a jagged, translucent look; and improves within hours of fluid therapy.
Permanent Scar: Usually in one eye (from a scratch or poke); has a distinct, solid white "dense" center; and does not change with hydration. 


METABOLIC BONE DISEASE (MBD):
- One of the most serious and sadly common conditions in captive squirrels
- Caused by: all-nut/seed diet, lack of UV light, calcium/phosphorus imbalance, improper diet
- Signs: limbs that bend easily, pathological fractures, seizure-like activity, tremors, difficulty climbing, knuckling of the feet, "rubber legs"
- Prevention is critical: balanced diet with rodent block, calcium supplement, UVB lighting
- Treatment: correct diet immediately, calcium supplementation, vet care
- Recovery is possible if caught early; severe cases may result in permanent damage
- NEVER feed squirrels primarily seeds, nuts, or corn — these cause MBD
-Primarly affects older squirrels who have weaned off of formula and are fed an inappropriate diet. See https://sites.google.com/view/thesquirrelbible/treatment-protocols/metabolic-bone-disease

URI (Upper Respiratory Infection):
- Signs: sneezing, nasal discharge (clear to yellow/green), eyes runny or crusty
- May be viral (supportive care) or bacterial (antibiotics from vet)
- Supportive: warm environment, fluid support, light nebulization with saline
- Contact vet if discharge becomes colored, thick, or baby is declining

PARASITES — INTERNAL:
- Coccidia: most common — see above
- Roundworms (Baylisascaris): rare but can be fatal; treat with Panacur (Fenbendazole) — vet dosing
- Giardia: watery diarrhea — diagnosed by fecal test; treated with Metronidazole — vet prescription

PARASITES — EXTERNAL:
- Botfly (Cuterebra): larvae under the skin — DO NOT SQUEEZE; carefully expand the breathing hole with a small amount of Vaseline and allow the larva to partially emerge, then grasp with forceps; never crush the larva inside (can cause anaphylaxis)
- Fleas: common in young wild-caught squirrels; use Revolution (Selamectin) — vet prescription — NEVER use dog/cat flea products
- Mange mites: hair loss, crusty skin; treated with Revolution or Ivermectin (never Ivermectin in neonates) — vet dosing
- Ringworm (fungal, not a worm): circular bald patches, crusty; treated with Fluconazole or antifungal cream — vet guidance

HYPOGLYCEMIA (LOW BLOOD SUGAR):
- Signs: sudden weakness, trembling, seizure, loss of consciousness
- Emergency: rub a tiny amount of Karo light corn syrup or honey on the gums immediately
- Do NOT put large amounts of sugar in the mouth of a seizing/unconscious animal (aspiration risk)
- Keep warm, call a vet immediately
- Follow with formula feeding once baby is conscious and stable

SEIZURES:
- Can be caused by: hypoglycemia, MBD, head trauma, toxin exposure, severe infection
- Immediate: darken the environment, keep very quiet and still, maintain warmth
- Apply a drop of Karo syrup to gums if hypoglycemia is suspected
- Contact a vet immediately — seizures require diagnosis and treatment
- Diazepam or Midazolam may be used by a vet to stop seizures

SHOCK:
- Signs: cold extremities, pale/white gums, labored breathing, unresponsive, limp
- Warm slowly and gently — do not overheat
- Keep very quiet and still, minimize handling
- Do NOT feed a baby in shock
- Contact a vet immediately — shock requires IV fluids and emergency treatment

HEAD TRAUMA / SPINAL INJURY:
- Common after falls or cat attacks
- Signs: unable to walk/climb, head tilt, circling, nystagmus (eye twitch) paralysis of limbs, unresponsive
- Keep the animal still, minimize movement, especially of the spine
- Darken environment, keep warm and quiet
- Vet evaluation is critical — X-rays and possibly anti-inflammatory medication needed
- Cage rest for weeks may be required; many squirrels recover from falls with time

WOUNDS & LACERATIONS:
- Cat/dog puncture wounds: even tiny wounds can be fatal due to Pasteurella bacteria
- ANY cat or dog wound requires immediate antibiotics — contact vet
- Clean wounds with dilute chlorhexidine (1:50 dilution with water) or saline
- Do NOT use hydrogen peroxide (damages tissue)
- Small wounds: cover with antibiotic ointment (plain Neosporin — no pain relief version)
- Burns: Silvadene cream (vet prescription) — keep moist and clean
- Maggots (fly strike): remove maggots with forceps, clean wound thoroughly, contact vet for antibiotics and wound care

URINE SCALD:
- Red, irritated, raw skin around genitals and inner thighs
- Caused by failure to properly stimulate/clean after feedings
- Treatment: clean area gently with warm water, apply a thin layer of plain petroleum jelly or Aquaphor
- Diaper rash cream (zinc oxide) can help but avoid heavy application near genitals
- Ensure thorough stimulation after every feeding

EYE INFECTIONS / CONJUNCTIVITIS:
- Red, swollen, weeping, or crusted eyes
- Clean gently with warm saline on a soft cloth
- Apply Terramycin ophthalmic ointment (available OTC at farm supply stores) 2–3x per day
- If eyes are swollen shut or discharge is copious, seek vet attention

DENTAL ISSUES:
- Overgrown or malocclusion incisors: common in bottle-raised squirrels
- Signs: difficulty eating, drooling, food dropping, weight loss
- Requires veterinary dental filing — do NOT attempt to cut teeth at home
- Provide hard items to chew: wooden toys, branches, hard nuts
- Broken teeth usually regrow in 6–8 weeks

SELF-MUTILATION:
- Squirrels may chew their own limbs or tail, especially after injury or nerve damage
- Can also be triggered by over-imprinting, boredom, or pain
- An e-collar (tiny Elizabethan collar) may be needed to prevent access to the wound
- Address underlying cause: pain management, enrichment, socialization with other squirrels
- Vet evaluation is needed if ongoing

## Section 5 — Medications Reference

⚠️ DOSING IS ALWAYS DETERMINED BY A VETERINARIAN. This is a general reference only.

ANTIBIOTICS:
- Baytril (Enrofloxacin): broad-spectrum; used for respiratory infections, wounds, UTIs; NOT for neonates
- Doxycycline: excellent for Bordetella and respiratory; NOTE: do NOT give with calcium-containing foods within 2 hours — blocks absorption
- Amoxicillin/Clavulanate (Clavamox): broad spectrum, good for wound infections
- Nuflor (Florfenicol): injectable; reserved for severe pneumonia/sepsis
- Trimethoprim-Sulfa (TMS/Bactrim): for UTIs, GI infections
- Metronidazole (Flagyl): anaerobic infections, Giardia

ANTIPARASITICS:
- Albon (Sulfadimethoxine): coccidia — given as a 10-day course typically
- Ponazuril: coccidia — often preferred for its shorter course
- Panacur (Fenbendazole): roundworms, Giardia
- Revolution (Selamectin): fleas, mites — ONLY vet-prescribed; NEVER use dog/cat flea products
- Ivermectin: mites — NOT safe for very young neonates

ANTI-INFLAMMATORIES / PAIN:
- Meloxicam (Metacam): NSAID; pain and inflammation; very common in wildlife rehab
  ⚠️ NEVER combine with other NSAIDs or corticosteroids (risk of GI ulceration)
- Gabapentin: nerve pain, chronic pain, seizure prevention
- Prednisone/Dexamethasone: corticosteroids for severe inflammation/shock
  ⚠️ NEVER combine with NSAIDs

SEDATIVES / ANXIETY:
- Trazodone: mild sedation, anti-anxiety; useful for transport stress or procedures
- Benadryl (Diphenhydramine): mild sedation, allergic reactions; very small doses

TOPICAL / OTC:
- Terramycin ophthalmic ointment: eye infections (OTC at farm supply stores)
- Silvadene cream: burns and severe wounds (prescription)
- Mylicon (Simethicone infant drops): gas and bloat — safe and effective
- Chlorhexidine dilute solution: wound cleaning
- Triple antibiotic ointment (plain Neosporin): minor wounds — never the "pain relief" version (Pramoxine is toxic)

ABSOLUTELY NEVER USE ON SQUIRRELS:
- Any dog or cat flea/tick product with the exception of Capstar Kitten (highly toxic)
- Permethrin or pyrethroid-based products
- Human adult pain medications (Tylenol/acetaminophen, Advil/ibuprofen, aspirin)
- Human cold/flu medications
- Adult-strength Imodium (loperamide)
- Essential oils (lavender, tea tree, eucalyptus — all toxic)
- Xylitol (sugar substitute — found in many products; extremely toxic)
- Ivermectin in very young neonates
-THERE ARE EXCEPTIONS TO EVERY RULE. WHEN IN DOUBT CONSULT THE SQUIRREL BIBLE ONLINE FOR A COMPREHENSIVE LIST OF EXCLUSIONS/RARE EXCEPTIONS AND 911 ALLOWABLE SWAPS: https://sites.google.com/view/thesquirrelbible

DANGEROUS DRUG INTERACTIONS:
- Doxycycline + calcium-rich foods: calcium blocks doxycycline absorption — separate by 2 hours
- NSAIDs (Meloxicam) + Corticosteroids (Prednisone): GI ulceration, life-threatening
- NSAID stacking (two NSAIDs together): kidney failure
- NSAIDs + Aminoglycosides (Gentamicin, Amikacin): renal failure
- Multiple sedatives together (sedation stacking): respiratory depression

## Section 6 — Release Protocols

RELEASE CRITERIA:
- Minimum age: 14 weeks, minimum weight: 250g (larger is better)
- Fully able to run, jump, climb, and balance
- Able to crack open hard nuts independently
- Shows fear response to humans and predators
- Healthy — no active medical issues
- Has been in outdoor cage exposed to weather and temperature fluctuation for minimum 2 weeks (4 weeks is ideal)

RELEASE LOCATION:
- Mature hardwood forest with oak, hickory, or other mast-producing trees
- Existing squirrel population preferred (can follow/learn from wild squirrels)
- Away from roads, buildings, known predator territories
- Near water source
- Permission obtained if on private land

SOFT RELEASE (PREFERRED):
- Squirrels should have been in pre-release for at least 2 weeks (ideally 4 weeks) by now with minimal interactions from humans
- Continue to ensure they have food and water for at least a month after opening the door for them
- Open the door to the pre-release cage in the morning and allow the teens to venture out and explore (it may take a few days, don't panic)
- At night, close the door to ensure any teens inside are safe in their nest boxes
- Be aware male squirrels tend to disappear fairly quickly after release and range out to establish new territories whereas females do tend to stick closer to home for longer and may actually establish the area as their home base
- To encourage them to stay, it's never a bad idea to install a squirrel friendly nesting box in the trees surrounding your home
- Once all the squirrels have left the pre-release enclosure and consistently stay away, it's safe to move it back inside for the next round of babies
- There's nothing wrong with continuing to set out food and water for your released kids as not only will they appreciate it, but the local wildlife will as well

HARD RELEASE (NOT PREFERRED BUT SOMETIMES NECESSARY):
- Open carrier/cage at release site and allow squirrel to exit on its own
- Place food at base of tree
- Only appropriate for older, fully prepared juveniles

PRE-RELEASE PREPARATION:
- Wild diet training: reduce domestic foods, increase forage items
- Predator recognition: introduce limited, safe exposure to predator sounds/scents
- Weather hardening: outdoor exposure to rain, cold, heat
- Socialization: releasing pairs or groups is strongly preferred (lone squirrels have higher mortality)
- Reduce human interaction to near zero for 4–6 weeks before release

SEASON CONSIDERATIONS:
- Best release window: spring through early fall - Ideally you want trees with full leaf cover to provide them with the best security and ability to hide from predators
- Avoid releasing during winter if possible — young squirrels need time to cache food and find shelter before cold weather makes it impossible
- An unprepared young squirrel released in winter has a greater chance of not surviving than a squirrel who has been held over until weather and conditions are more favorable
- LAST RESORT: If a fall/winter release is unavoidable, choose a site with existing squirrel population, provide warm protected shelter and supplement aggressively
